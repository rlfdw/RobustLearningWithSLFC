§ 01  Abstract

Abstract

Manipulation tasks often consist of subtasks, each representing a distinct skill. Mastering these skills is essential for robots, as it enhances their autonomy, efficiency, adaptability, and ability to collaborate with humans. Learning from demonstrations allows robots to rapidly acquire new skills without starting from scratch, with demonstrations typically sequencing skills to achieve tasks.

Behaviour cloning approaches to learning from demonstration commonly rely on mixture density network output heads to predict robot actions. In this work, we first reinterpret the mixture density network as a library of feedback controllers (or skills) conditioned on latent states. This arises from the observation that a one-layer linear network with different initialisations is functionally equivalent to a classical feedback controller with different gains.

We use these insights to derive a probabilistic graphical model that combines these elements, describing the skill discovery process as segmentation in the latent space, where each skill policy functions as a feedback control law in the latent space. Our approach significantly improves not only task success rate, but also robustness to observation noise when trained with perfect demonstrations. Our physical robot experiments further show that the induced robustness simplifies model deployment on robots.

§ 02  Model

Model architecture

An encoder maps observations to a latent state, a switching variable selects among a library of latent feedback controllers, and each controller drives the latent state toward its own goal with its own gain.

§ 03  Results

Results

Three evaluations, in increasing order of control over the experimental conditions: a scalable simulation benchmark, a physical robot deployment, and a controlled ablation.

Eval 01

Scalable evaluation — Franka Kitchen

  • PerformanceTrained on perfect demonstrations, the model improves on the mixture density network by 12% on average, matching or exceeding its success rate on every task.
  • RobustnessAcross observation noise scales of 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1, 2 and 3, the margin over the baseline averages 8%.
Eval 02

Physical evaluation — robot writing

  • ConvergenceThe explicit feedback control design gives stable convergence to a latent set-point, so the robot stops at the end of the task rather than continuing to draw.
  • DeploymentThe induced robustness reduces the tuning needed to move the policy onto hardware.
Eval 03

Controlled analysis — FetchPush

  • Upper boundGiven enough training samples and the optimal number of skills, the baseline reaches comparable performance — the gain is in efficiency, not ceiling.
  • Sample efficiencyPerformance holds as the sample size varies, matching or exceeding both the mixture density network and plain behaviour cloning.
  • SensitivityThe advantage persists across sample sizes, skill counts and noise levels rather than depending on a tuned operating point.

§ 04  Analysis

Where the robustness comes from

Fréchet distance between generated and training trajectories, measured for the baseline, the baseline with the feedback control design, and the baseline with both the feedback design and switching skill consistency.

  • Latent spaceThe encoder–decoder structure accounts for roughly 3% of the robustness gain on its own.
  • Switching KLThe switching KL divergence constraint raises robustness across skills, suggesting headroom for larger skill libraries.
  • Controller gainHigher-gain controllers are more robust, and the gap widens as the perturbation grows.
  • Bounded behaviourLatent controller goals and gains anchor the policy near the training distribution; switching reinforces that bound.
